Where should the retro module attach?

“Reentry module” here means the propulsion / cold-gas retro module, discarded before atmospheric entry, rather than the entry capsule itself. Compare a module beneath the windward heat shield with one above the aft top cover. Both must finish separation, retroburn alignment and entry alignment before module jettison. The top cover opens later for carrier extraction.

A · Beneath the heat shield

Current illustrated configuration. Needs a primary structural hardpoint, a TPS continuity solution, motor heat isolation and positive forward-side clearance before entry.

B · Above the top cover

Candidate for further design. Attach to a structural bridge or load-bearing cover frame, rather than an unreinforced lid. A bridge must release or remain outside the extraction corridor.

Diagrams are functional schematics. Forebody cone lines illustrate the NASA 70° axis half-angle convention; nose/shoulder rounding and backshell dimensions are not a released CAD design. The top location with an outward-facing nozzle reverses the thrust direction relative to the capsule, so its commanded burn attitude must be re-derived.

Recommendation and decision gates

Prefer a top-side structural-ring / bridge concept for the next CAD trade if it can carry retroburn thrust and fully clear the carrier extraction path. Preserving an uninterrupted heat-shield face is the principal reason. Do not select a simple top-cover attachment without a demonstrated load path. If top-side clearance or structural mass cannot close, retain the bottom option only with a qualified TPS interface and safe pre-entry separation. Neither option is currently demonstrated to be flightworthy.

Thrust alignment: location alone does not decide control demand

Moving an axially aligned module from bottom to top does not itself create thrust torque. The perpendicular distance e between the thrust line and combined center of mass does: τ = F e. For constant torque before a controller responds, Δθ = ½(τ/I)t². This short-interval rigid-body screen excludes flexure, nozzle cant evolution, propellant motion and active control. It does not calculate cold-gas consumption.

These are separate illustrative assumptions: 100 N and 1.5 kg m² mirror the initial A trade; 1 mm and 0.1 s are analyst-selected sensitivity inputs. They are not synchronized to flight sliders.

What must close before selecting a mount?

  1. Structure and mass: model the ring, cover frame, bridge, bolts and release devices under axial/lateral thrust, launch loads and separation shock. Compare actual reinforced masses and combined center of mass/inertia over propellant depletion. Include proof margins; do not count an unreinforced lid as a thrust structure.
  2. Thermal protection: show a smooth, continuous entry forebody after bottom release, or a qualified closure. For both mounts evaluate motor plume, soakback, backshell heating and bioscience sample temperatures and contamination.
  3. Guidance and gas: re-derive each of the three maneuvers with signed thrust-axis geometry and mounting-specific inertia. The current 90° retroburn and 180° entry alignment assumptions are not transferable evidence for the top option. Include rates, dispersions and cold-gas reserve.
  4. Separation: run a six-degree-of-freedom clearance / recontact study from jettison through entry, including failed or partial release, springs, residual thrust, rotation and aerodynamics. Positive initial release speed alone is insufficient.
  5. Deployment and software: inhibit entry readiness until release is confirmed or a defined fault response is executed; prevent cover opening and parachute deployment into uncleared hardware. Verify separation sensing, power and harness disconnects, then carrier extraction in a representative test.

How far aft can the CG be?

Define axial position x from the forebody nose toward the top cover, positive aft; “higher CG” means farther aft, not altitude above Mars. D is the live heat-shield diameter. This study applies to the retained entry capsule after retro module jettison. Its actual CG must be calculated from retained component masses and positions: xCG = Σ(mi xi)/Σmi. The current mass allocation has no component station data, so CG below is an assumption, not an output of the mass table. Radial CG offset, trim angle and load movement require separate analysis.

For a small-disturbance static screen, use an effective neutral point xNP defined by the moment derivative, rather than treating a drag center as a universal pressure point. Under the convention CNα > 0, Cmα = −CNα (xNP − xCG)/D. A restoring moment requires Cmα < 0. Static margin SM = (xNP − xCG)/D. Set a minimum margin, then xCG,max/D = min(xNP/D across regimes) − 3σNP/D − SMrequired.

Every neutral point, uncertainty and margin below is illustrative. They are not NASA-derived coefficients or a fitted probability distribution. The “3σ” subtraction is a sensitivity allowance on assumed neutral-point uncertainty only; it is not a 99.7% mission-stability claim. It omits CG measurement error and correlations. The minimum of three assumed regime values does not cover every Mach, angle of attack or rarefied-flow condition.

Assumed regimeNominal static margin

With the initial assumptions, the limiting aft CG is 0.19D, or 0.152 m at D = 0.8 m. This is a worked sensitivity case, not a recommended flight CG. A negative maximum CG indicates no feasible nonnegative CG under the selected assumptions. Lower/forward CG generally increases this static margin; it does not automatically provide pitch damping or prevent tumbling.

Dynamic stability remains open: a statically restoring capsule can have growing pitch oscillations. Obtain Cmα and pitch damping (Cmq + Cmα̇) versus Mach, angle of attack and rarefaction, then run six-degree-of-freedom dispersions through entry and parachute deployment with as-built mass properties. Include mount separation errors, radial offset and inertia. NASA’s MER ballistic-range tests tested CG positions 0.27, 0.30 and 0.33D aft of the nose and found increasing dynamic instability with aft movement. Those positions are test cases, not transferable FREYA limits. MSL dynamic-stability tests characterized pitch damping over a defined Mach/angle envelope, illustrating the additional data needed.

How thick must the heat shield be?

Use heat flux and exposure time, not peak surface temperature alone. This study reads the current flight’s entry rows (qCenter, qShoulder, t) and replays the same two-node thermal model at each thickness. It updates when configuration, trajectory, atmosphere, heating geometry or thermal material settings change. It does not infer PICA or SLA-561V properties from a generic material.

The 1.5× flux factor, 300 s soak, 0 residual flux, 1500°C surface limit and 50°C bulk limit are analyst-selected sensitivity assumptions, not certified material or bioscience limits. The live entry ends at the chosen carrier extraction altitude; continued atmospheric heating is not propagated here. Zero-flux soak explores stored-heat diffusion with surface radiation and can understate exposure if heating continues. A finite soak interval can miss a later peak. Bulk temperature is a lumped internal TPS node, not an actual back-face, bondline, cabin or sample temperature.

Equations, assumptions and relationship to the flight model

At each center/shoulder station: Cs = ρ Cp δ; Cb = ρ Cp (L − δ); G = k/(L/2). dTs/dt = [fq(t) − εσ(Ts⁴ − Tinitial⁴) − G(Ts − Tb)]/Cs; dTb/dt = G(Ts − Tb)/Cb. δ is the live surface-layer thickness, L is the candidate total thickness, and σ = 5.670374419×10⁻⁸ W/(m² K⁴). The flight model’s explicit substeps are at most 0.025 s. Entry replay uses each interval’s ending heat-flux sample, matching the existing solver. Soak uses the specified residual flux at both stations. Reported maxima include both stations and the whole simulated interval.

The sweep evaluates 5–80 mm in 5 mm steps, skipping L ≤ δ. “First candidate” means the thinnest sampled value meeting both assumed limits; it is not a continuous optimum or manufacturing specification. The chart shows the current flight thickness, not the suggested candidate. An entry factor of 1 and soak of 0 reproduce the current flight’s two-node temperature outputs. Changing candidate thickness holds the trajectory and heat-flux history fixed, so additional shield mass is not fed back into drag, CG, inertia or the mass budget.

Approximate TPS mass = ρ L Acone, with Acone = πD²/(4 cos20°), using a straight-cone surface area at the NASA forebody angle. This excludes nose/shoulder blending, seams, adhesive, structure, backshell and margin. It is not a mass allocation. Geometry and material uncertainties can dominate.

What this cannot establish: the generic nonablating, constant-property model has no pyrolysis, recession, char, anisotropy, bondline, attachment hot spots, transition-to-turbulence uncertainty or thermomechanical failure. A temperature below a placeholder limit does not demonstrate survival. Increasing thickness may protect the bulk while barely reducing the surface peak; if no thickness meets a surface criterion, reconsider material and exposure rather than assuming more thickness solves it.

Next design gate: select a flight-lot material and allowable bondline/sample limits, develop dispersed heating/pressure histories through the relevant mission interval, solve temperature-dependent multilayer ablation and recession, add residual-thickness and uncertainty margins, close mass/CG iteratively, and validate with arc-jet and structural tests. NASA’s MEDLI2 ablator models describe flight-lot material-response validation. NASA’s PICA overview explains how ablation dissipates heat. Those sources justify using a material-response workflow; they do not validate FREYA’s generic thermal constants.

Cold-gas consumption versus tank capacity

Three cold-gas maneuvers: separation translation, retroburn attitude alignment, and post-burn heat-shield entry alignment. The cold-gas module remains attached through both turns and is jettisoned five seconds after entry alignment ends. Default 90°/180° angles are planning assumptions, not an attitude solution. Impact equations, assumptions and sources

Poncelet screen: resisting force F = a + b v²; a = area × effective bearing resistance; b = 0.5 × density × inertial coefficient × area. Penetration D = m/(2b) ln(1 + b v²/a); peak deceleration = (a + b v²)/(m g₀); mean deceleration = v²/(2Dg₀). Stopping time = m/√(ab) atan(v√(b/a)). Normal speed uses the instantaneous downward probe velocity and an adjustable obliquity. Gravity during the short impact, nose evolution, layering, strain rate, fracture, shoulder engagement, probe bending and energy absorbed by the probe are omitted.

Assumed soft sand: R 0.20 MPa, density 1,600 kg/m³, coefficient 0.8. Hard soil: R 3.0 MPa, density 2,000 kg/m³, coefficient 1.0. Sandstone: R 25 MPa, density 2,300 kg/m³, coefficient 1.2. Effective bearing resistance is not unconfined compressive strength. Test calibration is required for each site and probe.

The heat-shield forebody shall use a 70 degree cone half-angle from the symmetry axis, equivalent to 20 degrees from the shield reference plane, consistent with NASA Mars sphere-cone examples. Backshell geometry shall be sized independently.

Compare matrix blanks, inactivated controls, reference spikes and representative simulants. Use measured sample recovery and tracer accounting, including isotope exchange, before assigning a cause to excess counts.
